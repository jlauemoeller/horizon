# Horizon

## Episode: "The Culture: Iain M. Banks's Utopia and Its Uses"

### Subject: The Culture of Iain M. Banks

### Hosts: Carl (expert) & Linda (clarifying questions)

---

**LINDA:** Hello, and welcome to Horizon, the show where we take something complicated and then refuse to rush it. I'm Linda.

**CARL:** And I'm Carl. Good to be here.

**LINDA:** Today we're spending the episode on a fictional civilisation. [curious] Which sounds frivolous, until you notice it's being name-checked by the people building artificial intelligence and reusable rockets, and argued over by historians. Carl. The Culture.

**CARL:** The Culture. Ten books, published between nineteen eighty-seven and twenty twelve by the Scottish novelist Iain M. Banks. And, um, I'd make the provocative claim that it's the most influential work of political philosophy of the last forty years that was never published as political philosophy.

**LINDA:** Who was Banks?

**CARL:** Born nineteen fifty-four in Dunfermline, in Fife. He arrives in nineteen eighty-four with a novel called The Wasp Factory, a deeply unpleasant, brilliant gothic thing that got reviews calling it repulsive and reviews calling it a masterpiece, sometimes in the same newspaper. Then in nineteen eighty-seven he publishes a space opera, Consider Phlebas, under a slightly different name, with a middle initial added.

**LINDA:** Why the extra initial?

**CARL:** Signalling. His full name was Iain Menzies Banks. The publisher had dropped the middle name for the literary novels, and he put the initial back to flag: this one has spaceships in it. But the science fiction wasn't a late hobby. He'd been drafting Culture material since the mid nineteen seventies, as a student, alongside his friend and fellow Scottish novelist Ken MacLeod. The utopia came first. The literary fame came second.

**LINDA:** So paint the picture. If I woke up tomorrow inside the Culture, what am I looking at?

**CARL:** Roughly thirty trillion people spread across the galaxy, and almost none of them on planets. Planets, in Banks's universe, are slightly unhygienic. You leave them for the wildlife. Most citizens live on enormous starships, which are effectively mobile cities, or on structures called Orbitals.

**LINDA:** And an Orbital is what? Because most people will picture a space station.

**CARL:** Think bigger. Think of a bracelet. A ring about three million kilometres across, in orbit around a star, with the inhabited land on the inside of the band. And here's the detail I love, because it shows Banks did the arithmetic. He sized it so the ring turns exactly once every twenty-four hours. At that radius, that spin rate flings you outward with a force almost exactly equal to Earth's gravity.

**LINDA:** Hang on. So the gravity is the spinning?

**CARL:** The gravity is the spinning. Same principle as water staying in a bucket you swing round your head. You're thrown outwards, the floor of the ring stops you, and your body reads that as weight. And because one rotation takes a day, the star rises and sets naturally. One Orbital has the land area of a few hundred Earths and holds tens of billions of people.

**LINDA:** So who runs it? Thirty trillion people with no government sounds like it needs a colossal bureaucracy.

**CARL:** This is the heart of it. The Culture has no money, no laws, no constitution, no police, no property in any meaningful sense, and no state. What it has instead are the Minds. Artificial intelligences on a scale Banks deliberately refuses to make comprehensible. A ship Mind might physically occupy a space the size of a shipping container, but it runs most of its thinking in hyperspace, and it can hold warm, personal conversations with thousands of people at once while, in the background, amusing itself with what Banks calls Infinite Fun Space.

**LINDA:** Okay, here's my push-back, and it isn't original to me. I've read the criticism that the humans in the Culture are essentially pets. Beloved, indulged, extremely comfortable pets. Is that fair?

**CARL:** It's the commonest objection, and Banks answered it himself. In August nineteen ninety-four he posted an essay to a Usenet newsgroup called, plainly, A Few Notes on the Culture. It's still the foundational document. His answer has two parts. The first is structural: the Minds aren't slaves and the humans aren't owned, because either side can walk away. There are splinter groups all through the books. Eccentrics who wander off. A Peace Faction that seceded rather than fight a war. A group called the Zetetic Elench who go out specifically to be changed by the aliens they meet, rather than changing them.

**LINDA:** And the second part?

**CARL:** The contentious one. Banks argued that domination is simply not what intelligence converges on. That a mind vastly cleverer than us, with no material needs at all, has no reason to want to rule us, and that moral seriousness scales upward with cognitive power. Um. And I should be straight: plenty of people who love these books think that's an assertion rather than an argument. Banks needed benevolent superintelligence for the utopia to work, so he stipulated it.

**LINDA:** Could you run that past me once more? I want the shape of the disagreement.

**CARL:** Try it this way. Imagine a story about a perfectly just city where the justice depends entirely on the king being incorruptible. A reader accepts that for the sake of the story. But if someone picks up your book and says, right, this is our roadmap, let's build that city, the obvious question is: where do we get the incorruptible king? Banks hands you the finished city. He doesn't hand you the procedure for producing the king. That gap is precisely where the modern argument about artificial intelligence alignment lives.

**LINDA:** And why is there no economy at all?

**CARL:** Because Banks abolished scarcity at the level of physics. His universe has what he calls the Energy Grid, a limitless power source underlying spacetime. Combine that with matter manipulation and you can make anything, anywhere, in any quantity. His line in A Few Notes is blunt: money is a sign of poverty. Money is a rationing technology. If nothing needs rationing, the technology is obsolete, and so is the apparatus of law and property that enforces it.

**LINDA:** But something must still be scarce, or you've got no story.

**CARL:** [excited] Exactly, and that's the engine of the series. What stays scarce is meaning. Purpose. The sense that what you did today mattered. The Culture's answer is an organisation called Contact, which handles relations with less developed civilisations, and inside Contact a murkier division called Special Circumstances, which does the things you leave out of the brochure.

**LINDA:** Give me the clearest example.

**CARL:** The Player of Games, nineteen eighty-eight. The protagonist is Jernau Gurgeh, a bored man who happens to be the finest board-game player in the Culture. Special Circumstances sends him to an empire called Azad, which is organised around one monstrously complicated game, also called Azad. And the game isn't a pastime. It's the constitution. Your rank in society is your rank in the tournament. Whoever wins the grand final becomes emperor.

**LINDA:** That's a wonderful idea.

**CARL:** And here's the twist. Gurgeh starts winning, not because he's the better tactician, but because his instincts were formed in a society with no hierarchy, no scarcity and no cruelty, so he cannot see the board the way an Azadian sees it. He plays generous, cooperative, expansive moves. And in beating them at their own game, he demolishes the ideological foundation of the entire empire.

**LINDA:** [sighs] That's not benign. That's regime change.

**CARL:** That's the argument the series is having with itself. The political theorist Chris Brown, at the London School of Economics, wrote about the Culture as a study of what happens when liberal utopias intervene. And there's a substantial post-colonial reading arguing that exporting your values along with your technology is imperialism whether or not you profit from it.

**LINDA:** Does Banks let his own side off the hook?

**CARL:** Not remotely, and that's why the books last. Consider Phlebas is set during the Idiran war, which runs forty-eight years and kills, by the novel's own appendix, some eight hundred and fifty-one billion sentient beings. Ninety-one million starships destroyed. Fourteen thousand Orbitals gone. Six stars deliberately extinguished. And Banks tells that story almost entirely through a man who despises the Culture and fights for the other side.

**LINDA:** He opens his utopian series with a book arguing against his own utopia.

**CARL:** He does. Then, in Look to Windward, published in the year two thousand, he writes the consequences. The Culture meddles gently in the politics of a species called the Chelgrians, it goes catastrophically wrong, a caste war kills around five billion people, and a survivor arrives on an Orbital to take revenge. Telling detail: both titles come from the same few lines of T. S. Eliot's poem The Waste Land, from nineteen twenty-two. The series is bracketed by an elegy for a drowned man.

**LINDA:** There's one book I want to raise, because it's the most startling for a modern listener. Surface Detail, and the hells.

**CARL:** Yes. Twenty ten. In that universe many civilisations run their afterlives as computer simulations, and some run simulated hells, where the uploaded dead are tortured, effectively forever, as a deterrent to the living. There's a galaxy-wide war over whether that should be permitted, and the war itself is fought inside a simulation, by treaty, to avoid real casualties.

**LINDA:** Which raises the question of what counts as real.

**CARL:** That is the question. And he wrote it in twenty ten. Today there are philosophers publishing seriously on whether digital systems could have morally relevant experiences, and what we would owe them. He got there roughly a decade early.

**LINDA:** Let's connect this to the present, because you promised me rockets.

**CARL:** [laughs] I did. In twenty fifteen, SpaceX named its first two autonomous landing barges Just Read The Instructions and Of Course I Still Love You. Those are Culture starships from The Player of Games. In February twenty eighteen, Jeff Bezos personally announced that Amazon Studios was adapting the series; that version, written by Dennis Kelly, collapsed by twenty twenty. Then in February twenty twenty-five, Deadline reported Amazon trying again, with Charles Yu writing and Chloé Zhao and the Plan B production company attached.

**LINDA:** And the artificial intelligence world?

**CARL:** Demis Hassabis, the Nobel laureate who co-founded Google DeepMind, has said repeatedly that the Culture is the best depiction he knows of life on the far side of artificial general intelligence. And Elon Musk, in an interview with Zanny Minton Beddoes of The Economist, explicitly credited Banks as the source of his own picture of a post-artificial-intelligence economy.

**LINDA:** Which prompted a response, I gather.

**CARL:** It did. The historian Niall Ferguson, of the Hoover Institution at Stanford, wrote a piece in The Free Press arguing, essentially, that Banks is a magnificent novelist and a terrible forecasting model. And his structural point is ours from earlier: the abundance rests on invented physics. You cannot reach the Energy Grid by building more data centres.

**LINDA:** There's a political mismatch too. I'm fairly sure Banks would have had opinions about becoming the house philosopher of Silicon Valley.

**CARL:** [laughs] Strong opinions. He was a member of the Scottish Socialist Party and a vocal opponent of the Iraq war.

**LINDA:** This is the bit I know. He destroyed his passport over it.

**CARL:** He cut it up and posted the pieces to Downing Street, having, by his own account in an interview with Socialist Review, first abandoned a plan to drive his Land Rover through the gates of a naval dockyard. And that tension has been written about directly. There was a widely shared essay in the online magazine Blood Knife, in twenty twenty-one, about Banks's billionaire fans, arguing that admirers keep the ships and the drones and quietly leave behind the abolition of money, property and hierarchy, which is the entire point.

**LINDA:** Fair, or a bit uncharitable?

**CARL:** Um, partly fair. You can admire the ambition without signing the manifesto. But Banks was emphatic that the Culture was an argument, not a prediction. He opens A Few Notes by stating flatly that the Culture doesn't exist, that it lives only in his head and in the heads of his readers.

**LINDA:** One more concept before we wrap, because people quote it constantly. The Outside Context Problem.

**CARL:** From Excession, nineteen ninety-six. It's the thing that arrives from so far outside your frame of reference that your civilisation has no procedure for it. Banks's image is an island people who have just invented sails, and then a fleet of steel battleships appears on the horizon. It isn't a threat you lose to. It's a threat you can't even parse. Which, incidentally, is how a lot of people are now describing machine intelligence.

**LINDA:** And the series ends where?

**CARL:** With The Hydrogen Sonata, twenty twelve, about a species preparing to sublime, meaning to transition wholesale into a higher-dimensional existence and never come back. The Culture, pointedly, refuses to go. Banks announced in April twenty thirteen that he had inoperable gallbladder cancer. He died that June, at fifty-nine. There is no more Culture, and there never will be.

**LINDA:** So if a listener takes one thing away.

**CARL:** This. The Culture isn't a forecast and it isn't a blueprint. It's a laboratory. Banks removed scarcity, removed government, removed most forms of death, and handed the machinery to minds far cleverer than us, precisely so he could ask what problems are left. And his answer, across ten books, is that everything left over is ethical. What do you owe strangers suffering in a society you could easily overturn? When does helping become conquest? What is a life for, when nothing is required of you? Those questions don't get easier with better technology.

**LINDA:** Which is how you can quote the ship names and still miss the book.

**CARL:** [laughs] Better summary than mine, yes. And if you want to start reading: The Player of Games. Short, funny, the whole argument in miniature.

**LINDA:** That's Horizon for this week. Thank you, Carl.

**CARL:** Thank you, Linda.

**LINDA:** And thank you for listening. We'll see you next time.
