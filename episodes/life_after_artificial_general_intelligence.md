# Horizon

## Episode: "Life After Artificial General Intelligence"

### Subject: What does a post-AGI society look like?

### Hosts: Linda (expert) & Carl (clarifying questions)

---

**CARL:** Welcome to Horizon, the podcast where we take a genuinely complicated subject and give it the time it actually deserves. I'm Carl.

**LINDA:** And I'm Linda. Good to be back in the studio.

**CARL:** Today we're doing something slightly unusual. Instead of explaining something that has already happened, we're going to try to think rigorously about something that hasn't. The question is: what would a society look like after artificial general intelligence? Not the science fiction version with the glowing red eyes. The economics, the politics, and the ordinary day-to-day texture of it.

**LINDA:** Right, and, um, let me put a fence around this at the very start. Nobody knows. But nobody knows is not the same thing as anything goes. There is now a serious research literature here — economists, political scientists, philosophers — and in the last two years it has become a lot more specific. So we can at least map the disagreements honestly.

**CARL:** Let's start with the words themselves. What do people actually mean by artificial general intelligence? Because I have a suspicion that half of the argument is definitional.

**LINDA:** It really is. OpenAI's founding charter defines it roughly as highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most economically valuable work — notice, that's an economic definition, not a cognitive one. Demis Hassabis, the chief executive of Google DeepMind, uses a cognitive definition, a system that can do essentially anything the human brain can do, and he has consistently put it at something like a fifty percent chance by 2030. Dario Amodei at Anthropic more or less refuses the term altogether; he prefers to talk about powerful AI. So you have a single phrase that bundles a capability claim and an economic claim together, and people slide between the two without noticing.

**CARL:** So when two experts argue about whether we get there in 2027 or in 2040, they may not even be arguing about the same thing.

**LINDA:** Frequently, yes. Which is why I find measurement more useful than definitions. There's a nonprofit evaluation group called METR that publishes something they call a time horizon. They time human experts doing real software and research tasks, then ask: how long a task can a model finish with fifty percent reliability? The headline finding, from their 2025 paper, is that this length has been doubling roughly every seven months since 2019. They put out an expanded version in January of this year with a bigger task suite, and, importantly, there's also a preprint from February arguing the trend may not be cleanly exponential at all. So even the measurement is contested.

**CARL:** Can you run the time horizon idea past me once more? I want to be able to picture it in my head.

**LINDA:** Sure. Picture a ladder lying on the ground, and each rung is a job of a certain length. Bottom rung: a two-minute task. Next rung up: twenty minutes. Then two hours. Then a full working day. Now imagine water rising up that ladder. On today's frontier systems the water is somewhere around the low-hours mark: near-perfect on things that take a skilled human four minutes, but below ten percent success on things that take four hours. Everything in the post-AGI conversation depends on how fast that water keeps rising, and whether there's a ceiling somewhere.

**CARL:** Okay. And in the actual economy, right now, in 2026 — is any of this visible in the jobs numbers?

**LINDA:** Faintly, and it's disputed. The most cited study is Canaries in the Coal Mine, by Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen at the Stanford Digital Economy Lab. Using anonymised payroll records covering millions of American workers, they find employment declines concentrated among young workers in highly exposed occupations — software developers aged twenty-two to twenty-five are down roughly twenty percent from their peak, while their older colleagues in the same occupations are flat or growing. They published an update in February responding to their critics.

**CARL:** Now, I read something that pushed back on that. The Budget Lab at Yale looked at the whole economy and found the occupational mix is shifting no faster than it did when the personal computer or the internet arrived. Which sounds like a fairly direct contradiction.

**LINDA:** It's not quite a contradiction, but you're right that it's real tension. Those are different lenses: one is a microscope on exposed young workers, the other is a wide-angle shot of the whole labour market. The Peterson Institute for International Economics put it well in a piece in May — this research is, in their phrase, still in the first inning. Meanwhile a Stanford policy brief from July notes unemployment for new graduates hit five point six percent in early 2026, up about one and a half points in three years, and Goldman Sachs expects overall American unemployment around four and a half percent this year. That is a soft market with pockets of pain. It is not an apocalypse.

**CARL:** So the present is ambiguous. Which means the post-AGI question is genuinely a leap into a different regime.

**LINDA:** Exactly, and that's the honest framing. So — three camps. Camp one is the sceptics, and the standard-bearer is Daron Acemoglu at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. In his paper The Simple Macroeconomics of AI, he works task by task: what fraction of work is exposed, what fraction of that is actually profitable to automate, how big is the cost saving. He gets total factor productivity gains of no more than about zero point six six percent over ten years, and he argues even that may be generous.

**CARL:** Hold on — is he saying general intelligence won't arrive, or that it won't matter as much as people think?

**LINDA:** Mostly the second, plus scepticism about the premise. His model is about the technology we can actually see diffusing through firms, not about a hypothetical machine that does everything. Critics say that's precisely the limitation. Which brings us to camp two: Anton Korinek at the University of Virginia, with Donghyun Suh, in a paper called Scenarios for the Transition to AGI. Their picture is the rising water again. Imagine every task in the economy arranged by difficulty, easiest at the bottom. Automation is water rising. If the set of tasks is bounded, the water eventually covers all of them.

**CARL:** And their finding is that wages rise and then fall off a cliff? Can you say that a different way, because that feels backwards to me.

**LINDA:** [laughs] It does feel backwards, yes. Here's the intuition. As long as there is even one thing only humans can do, everything the machines do makes that human thing more valuable — the machines are complements, they're feeding you work. So wages boom. But the moment the water covers that last rung, your labour stops being scarce. Your wage is no longer set by what you can do. It's set by what it would cost the economy not to use you. And wages can be climbing right up until the day before that happens, which is why the transition gives you almost no warning.

**CARL:** That's genuinely unsettling. And camp three?

**LINDA:** Camp three takes that logic to its conclusion. Pascual Restrepo, an economist at Yale, has a chapter with the wonderfully bleak title We Won't Be Missed, in the volume The Economics of Transformative AI, edited by Ajay Agrawal, Erik Brynjolfsson and Anton Korinek for the University of Chicago Press. He splits work into bottleneck tasks — the things growth genuinely can't proceed without — and supplementary tasks. His conclusion: the economy automates all the bottleneck work, output becomes essentially linear in how much computing power you have, and human wages converge to the opportunity cost of the compute needed to reproduce your work. The labour share of national income converges towards zero. Concretely: your salary becomes the electricity-and-silicon price of the machine that would otherwise do your job.

**CARL:** This is where people always reach for the horses, isn't it? Horses were the engine of the economy, the American horse population peaked around 1915, and then the internal combustion engine simply retired them.

**LINDA:** It's the right analogy to reach for and it has one enormous flaw, which is worth sitting with. Horses did not own anything. Horses did not vote. Humans are simultaneously workers, owners and voters, and only the first of those three roles is threatened by automation. That's the hinge the entire debate turns on. And there's also a technical counterweight from Charles Jones and Christopher Tonetti at Stanford, in a working paper on how weak links tame the growth explosion. The old Baumol insight: if your growth requires a thousand steps and you automate nine hundred and ninety-nine of them, your speed is set by the one you didn't.

**CARL:** So the brake is the physical world. Energy, land, planning permission, supply chains.

**LINDA:** Energy, atoms, regulation and trust. Which is why the optimistic and pessimistic visions can both be internally consistent. On the optimistic side there's Dario Amodei's essay Machines of Loving Grace from October 2024, where he describes what he calls the compressed twenty-first century — the idea that we make a hundred years of biomedical progress in five or ten. On the pessimistic side there's an essay series from April 2025 by Luke Drago and Rudolf Laine called The Intelligence Curse. They borrow from the resource curse in development economics: when a state's revenue comes out of the ground rather than out of its citizens' labour, the incentive to educate, protect and listen to those citizens weakens. Their worry is that revenue from data centres behaves like oil.

**CARL:** So the nightmare isn't machines killing us. It's being very politely made irrelevant.

**LINDA:** That's precisely the framing in a position paper by Jan Kulveit and colleagues, presented at the International Conference on Machine Learning in 2025, called Gradual Disempowerment. Their argument is structural. Three big systems — the economy, culture, and the state — have historically depended on human participation, and that dependence is what forces them to serve human interests. Weaken all three feedback loops at once and you get catastrophe with no villain and no single dramatic moment. And I'd note their critics on the alignment forums think this is less likely than plain old power concentration in a few hands.

**CARL:** Let me bring in the policy answer everyone reaches for: universal basic income. And here I actually have data. The OpenResearch unconditional cash study ran from late 2020 to late 2023, three thousand participants, a thousand of them receiving one thousand dollars a month. Results came out in 2024. Recipients spent more on food, rent and transport, gave substantially more to family, worked modestly fewer hours — something like an hour or so a week — and were choosier about jobs. Physical health barely moved.

**LINDA:** That's a fair summary, and it's the best evidence we have — but I'd caution that it answers a different question than the one we're asking. That study tests how people behave when they get cash in an economy where labour still pays, and where the money came from philanthropic donors. The post-AGI question isn't behavioural, it's fiscal and political: where does the money come from when payroll taxes shrink? Korinek's work is good on this — our tax systems are built on wages. The proposals on the table now include the windfall clause from the Centre for the Governance of AI, a binding commitment to share profits above something like one percent of world output; taxing compute directly; sovereign-wealth-style national funds; and, as of this year, actual electoral politics — Alex Bores, a congressional candidate in New York, campaigned in April on what he calls an AI dividend.

**CARL:** And presumably there's a design argument about cash versus services.

**LINDA:** There is, and it's underrated. Pure cash preserves market signals, but if everyone's income rises and land is still scarce, a lot of it gets absorbed by rent. The alternative, associated with proposals like universal basic infrastructure and income, guarantees access to specific things — healthcare, education, connectivity, and, interestingly, compute itself. The argument being that in that world, access to compute is access to the means of production, and a voucher for that is worth more than the equivalent cash.

**CARL:** Which leaves the question I suspect most listeners are actually sitting with. If the machines do everything — what are people for?

**LINDA:** [sighs] Yeah. The book-length treatment is Nick Bostrom's Deep Utopia, from March 2024. He asks us to assume we solved everything — the safety problem, the distribution problem, all of it — and then asks what's left. He calls it a solved world, and argues we'd enter a post-instrumental condition where nothing you do is needed, only wanted. The reviews were mixed; the Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews called it provocative but sprawling, and the economist Robin Hanson complained, fairly bluntly, that after five hundred pages of build-up Bostrom never quite answers his own question.

**CARL:** Here's my hopeful counter-example. Everybody said chess would die when Deep Blue beat Kasparov in 1997. Instead chess is more popular now than at any point in its history, and nobody cares that a phone can beat the world champion.

**LINDA:** [excited] That's a genuinely good counter-example, and I think it's evidence for something real: we care about human-scale striving, watched by other humans. But I'd temper it. Chess is a bounded game with fixed rules and no material stakes. Nobody's rent depends on the Sicilian Defence. Extrapolating from a hobby to an entire civilisation's sense of purpose is a big jump. And history should make us humble here. John Maynard Keynes, writing in 1930, predicted a fifteen-hour working week for his grandchildren by 2030. He was roughly right about output per person and completely wrong about the leisure. And in 1966, the commission President Johnson set up on technology and automation concluded the problem wasn't the machines at all, it was managing demand.

**CARL:** So the pattern is: we forecast capability reasonably well and forecast the social response terribly.

**LINDA:** That's my read, yes. So if I had to give listeners three dials to watch, they'd be these. One: capability — do those time horizons keep doubling, and does reliability hold up on long tasks? Two: diffusion — the ratio of augmentation to automation in real usage data, and whether entry-level hiring keeps eroding. For context, Anthropic's own economic index found augmentation had edged back ahead of automation in late 2025, at fifty-two percent against forty-five. And three: distribution — who owns the compute, how it's taxed, and whether any of these profit-sharing commitments ever become legally binding.

**CARL:** Let me try to pull this together. There's no agreement on when, or even on what the word means. The economics splits between a modest scenario where this is another useful technology, a transitional scenario where wages boom and then collapse, and a full scenario where the labour share of income goes to zero. The physical world may act as a brake. And the biggest risk in the pessimistic accounts isn't machines with intentions — it's institutions quietly losing their reason to care about us.

**LINDA:** That's it. And the thing I'd leave people with is this: almost nothing in that list is determined by the technology. The same capability, dropped into different institutions, produces the compressed twenty-first century or the intelligence curse. The phrase post-AGI society makes it sound like a destination we arrive at. It isn't. It's a set of choices about ownership, taxation and political voice — and those choices are much easier to make before the capability lands than after.

**CARL:** A properly sobering note to end on. Thank you, Linda. And thank you all for listening to Horizon.

**LINDA:** Thanks, Carl. See you on the horizon.
